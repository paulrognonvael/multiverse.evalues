    \item \textbf{Comment:} I have a feeling that the weighted sum-to-zero coding in Section 2 is incorrect. As defined, $g_{ik} = \rho_k$ for observations in subpopulation $k$ and $-(1 - \rho_k)$ otherwise, so the column sum is $n_k\rho_k - (n - n_k)(1 - \rho_k) = n(\rho_k^2 - (1 - \rho_k)^2) = n(2\rho_k - 1)$, which is zero only when $\rho_k = 1/2$. The claim that $\sum_i g_{ik} = 0$, and hence the identity that $\beta_j^{(l)}$ equals the average effect fails. The intended coding is presumably $g_{ik} = 1 - \rho_k$ for observations in subpopulation $k$ and $-\rho_k$ otherwise, which does sum to zero. The subsequent expression for $\beta_{jk}^{(l)}$ must be corrected accordingly. Please verify whether the code implements the stated or the corrected coding.
    
    \reply 
Thank you for pointing out this important issue, this was an oversight on our end when writing the paper. Indeed, to guarantee that $\sum_{i=1}^n g_{ik}=0$ the coding should be $g_{ik}= 1 - \rho_k$ for individuals in subpopulation $k$ and $g_{ik}= -\rho_k$ otherwise. Then, letting $n_k$ be the number of observations coming from subpopulation $k$ and $\rho_k = n_k/n$ be the corresponding proportion, 
\begin{align}
 \sum_{i=1}^n g_{ik}= n_k (1-\rho_k) + (n - n_k) (- \rho_k)= n_k \frac{n - n_k}{n} - (n-n_k) \frac{n_k}{n} = 0.
\nonumber
\end{align}
We corrected the paper accordingly. We also checked that there were no errors in the code and that our previous results were correct (which was the case).

    \item \textbf{Comment:} The obvious p-value-based competitor is missing. Benjamini-Yekutieli also controls FDR under arbitrary dependence and requires no nuisance parameter estimation. The paper compares e-value strategies only against each other. A comparison of e-BH against BY is needed.
    
    \reply Thank you for pointing this out, we agree that a deeper discussion of the relative merits of BY and e-value strategies was needed, as well as adding BY as an alternative strategy. Following your suggestion, we added BY to our numerical experiments. We also added the following discussion comparing FDR control with BY to that with e-variables.

Given that both procedures control the FDR under arbitrary dependence, one aspect to consider are their respective powers.
For the particular case of the e-BH procedure, \cite{wang_ruodu:2022} showed that e-BH can have higher power than BY in certain settings and lower on others, depending on how the e-variables are obtained and on their distribution.
To gain some intuition, denote by $K$ the total number of tests, by $P_{[k]}$ the $k^{th}$ ordered P-value and by $E_{[k]}$ the $k^{th}$ ordered e-variable.
Recall that BY uses the threshold 
\begin{align}
P_{[k]} \leq \frac{\alpha k}{K c(K)}
\nonumber
\end{align}
where $c(K)= \sum_{i=1}^K 1/i \approx \ln(K) + 0.577$ is the $K^{th}$ harmonic number. % and $\gamma \approx 0.577$ the Euler-Mascheroni constant. 
In contrast, e-BH sets the threshold
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{E_{[k]}} \leq \frac{\alpha k}{K}.
\nonumber
\end{align}

There are therefore two issues driving how these two thresholds behave. 
First, as pointed out by \cite{wang_ruodu:2022}, e-BHs avoid the $\ln(K)$ term in the denominator, hence its power scales better as $K$ grows. 
Second, for any fixed $K$ the relative powers depend on the magnitude of $1/E_{[k]}$ versus that of $P_{[k]}$. For example, for the mixture calibrator e-variable (which has a fairly competitive power in our experiments), we have that
\begin{align}
 E_{[k]}= \frac{1 - P_{[k]} + P_{[k]} \ln(P_{[k]})}{P_{[k]} (\ln P_{[k]})^2},
\nonumber
\end{align}
where the right-hand side converges to $1/[P_{[k]} (\ln P_{[k]})^2$ as $P_{[k]} \to 0$. Hence and roughly speaking, for small $P_{[k]}$, applying e-BH to the mixture calibrator implies a threshold
\begin{align}
 P_{[k]} (\ln P_{[k]})^2 \leq \frac{\alpha k}{K}.
\nonumber
\end{align}
Therefore, if $(\ln P_{[k]})^2 > \ln(K) + 0.577$ then BY has higher power for rejecting these hypotheses, and otherwise e-BH has higher power. Note however that, because of the step-up nature of e-BH and BY, the hypotheses that are actually rejected are driven by the largest e-variable and smallest P-value (respectively) satisfying the threshold. That is, the power of these procedures is not by the most extreme e-variables / P-values.

We also remark hat e-BH is not the most powerful FDR controlling procedure for e-variables. For example, the closed e-BH procedure discussed in our paper has strictly higher power than e-BH, but we discuss e-BH here as it leads to more tractable expressions that provide some insights.


Another consideration is that, beyond power comparisons, there are situations where e-variables can have appealing properties.
One such property that can be relevant for multiverse analysis is evidence aggregation over various hypotheses. Suppose that one considers a set $A$ of related hypotheses (such as the effect of a treatment on multiple related outcomes), one can obtain an overall e-variable $E_A$ by aggregating the e-variables from the individual hypotheses. A simple such choice is defining the overall e-variable as the average (or weighted average) of the e-variables for the individual hypotheses. If all the hypotheses in $A$ are true, then $\mathbb E[E_A] = \sum_{k \in A} \mathbb E[E_{[k]}] / |A| \leq 1$. This can be used to test the intersection null hypothesis, that is that all null hypotheses in $A$ are true.

Another property, albeit less relevant to standard multiverse applications, is the anytime validity of e-variables. This is useful in sequential settings where one decides to continue or stop experimentation based on the data that was observed so far, in a manner that was not pre-defined in advance. In such settings, P-values cannot be calibrated to control type I error, whereas e-variables continue to be valid, provided that they are constructed to satisfy certain martingale properties. Although we are not aware of applications that have made such uses for multiverse analysis, one could consider a setting where a multiverse analysis is conducted using an available dataset, and based on those findings one decides to gather additional data to gain more information on some of the specific treatments or outcomes. Assuming the additional data to be independent from the previous data, one easily define e-variables that combine the evidence across both datasets, for example by taking the products of the e-variables obtained in each set. As a word of caution however, a repeated application of e-variable multiplicity adjustments does not guarantee FDR control. For example, applying e-BH once all data has been obtained controls the FDR, but applying e-BH for the first dataset and for the combined dataset does not control the FDR.

    \item \textbf{Comment:} The authors report different conclusions to the original studies. A slightly more extensive discussion on why that is the case would be welcomed.

    \reply Thank you for the suggestion. We added the following discussion to the paper.

\color{blue}
The main reason why our findings differ from the original study in \cite{orben:2019} is that the latter used Specification Curve Analysis to estimate a median treatment effect across all outcomes and treatments (as well as also across all possible control covariate configurations). As shown in Figure \ref{fig:exp.mcs} there is significant heterogeneity of the effects across outcomes and treatments. For example, the point estimate for the association between high TV usage and most outcomes was negative (suggesting that TV usage might be associated with lower chances of poor mental well-being), whereas for social media and internet use the estimates were positive for self-assessed outcomes like depression and low self-steem and negative for parent-assessed outcomes like high peer problems and low pro-sociality. The median of these heterogeneous effects is close to 0, which led \cite{orben:2019} to conclude that the association between mental well-being and technology use was too small to warrant policy change. We argue that in the presence of strong treatment effect heterogeneity, summaries such as the median can mask the presence of important individual treatment effects.
We remark that there are observational studies \cite{valkenburg:2022}, randomized controlled trials \cite{allcott:2020,allcott:2022} and natural experiments \cite{braghieri:2022} indicating that there is a negative effect of social media on self-reported mental well-being, aligning with our findings.
