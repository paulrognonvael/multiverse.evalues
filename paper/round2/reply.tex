\section*{Reply to the Editor}


\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Editor:} Reviewers have now commented on your paper. You will see that they are advising that you revise your manuscript. If you are prepared to undertake the work required, I would be pleased to reconsider my decision. Please in order to accept the manuscript for the special issue, I ask authors to check thoroughly points raised by Reviewer 1, in particular points 1 and 2.

    \reply Thank you for the opportunity to revise the manuscript. We have thoroughly revised it along the directions suggested by the referees (and, in particular, Points 1-2 raised by Referee 1). 
In fact, the observations from the referees made us realize that we had previously taken an overly simplistic view of e-values that was almost entirely focused on multiplicity control. The revised paper views their use more broadly, and to reflect that we updated the title to ``The role of e-values in multiverse analysis'' (before we mentioned the term ``multiplicity control'' explicitly).
We now discuss at greater depth that e-values may also be appealing in multiverse analyses for their flexibility in allowing one to combine evidence across hypotheses, and across datasets and time. In fact, our updated results indicate that e-values often have limited interest purely in terms of FDR control, since the Benjamini-Yekutieli p-value adjustment also controls the FDR and usually exhibits higher statistical power (except in large multiverses where one tests many hypotheses). Hence, the main appeal of e-values may lie in their flexibility.

We illustrated these issues by extending our simulation study, where we considered multiverses of various sizes and we added the Benjamini-Yekutieli procedure as a benchmark. We also showed in our teenager application how to use e-values to obtain overall measures of evidence that a treatment has no effect on any outcome, and on combining the evidence provided by two independent studies.
Other significant changes include:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item  Fixing the error in the write-up of the sum-to-zero constraint pointed out by Referee 1

\item Clarifying the terminology of ``treatment effect'', in particular explaining that it does not necessarily refer to causal effects, discussing an alternative definition mentioned by the referees, and discussing under what conditions the effect could be claimed to be causal.
\item We added references to the introduction and to other places suggested by Referee 2, to better frame our work within the literature.

\item We added an analytical comparison of Benjamini-Yekutieli p-value adjustment and the e-BH procedure for e-values (Section 3). The comparison suggests in which settings either procedure may be expected to have higher statistical power, and these observations match what we observe in our empirical results: while the power of both procedures does not differ massively, BY is better for smaller multiverses, and e-values are better for larger multiverses.

\item We added a new simulation study in Section 4.2 that mimics more closely a multiverse analysis setting. It considers multiple outcomes, a mix of truly active and truly inactive treatments, and covariate values are drawn from the teenager data to reflect a dependence structure that could be found in a real application.
\end{itemize}

Please see below for a point-by-point reply to these and other issues raised by the referees.



\end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Comment:} I have a feeling that the weighted sum-to-zero coding in Section 2 is incorrect. As defined, $g_{ik} = \rho_k$ for observations in subpopulation $k$ and $-(1 - \rho_k)$ otherwise, so the column sum is $n_k\rho_k - (n - n_k)(1 - \rho_k) = n(\rho_k^2 - (1 - \rho_k)^2) = n(2\rho_k - 1)$, which is zero only when $\rho_k = 1/2$. The claim that $\sum_i g_{ik} = 0$, and hence the identity that $\beta_j^{(l)}$ equals the average effect fails. The intended coding is presumably $g_{ik} = 1 - \rho_k$ for observations in subpopulation $k$ and $-\rho_k$ otherwise, which does sum to zero. The subsequent expression for $\beta_{jk}^{(l)}$ must be corrected accordingly. Please verify whether the code implements the stated or the corrected coding.
    
    \reply 
Thank you for pointing this out, it was an oversight on our end when writing the paper. Indeed, to guarantee that $\sum_{i=1}^n g_{ik}=0$ the coding should be $g_{ik}= 1 - \rho_k$ for individuals in subpopulation $k$ and $g_{ik}= -\rho_k$ otherwise. Then, letting $n_k$ be the number of observations coming from subpopulation $k$ and $\rho_k = n_k/n$ be the corresponding proportion, 
\begin{align}
 \sum_{i=1}^n g_{ik}= n_k (1-\rho_k) + (n - n_k) (- \rho_k)= n_k \frac{n - n_k}{n} - (n-n_k) \frac{n_k}{n} = 0.
\nonumber
\end{align}
We corrected the paper accordingly. We also checked that there were no errors in the code and that our previous results were correct (which was the case). 

\vspace{0.5cm}

    \item \textbf{Comment:} The simulations never evaluate the multiverse setting the paper is motivated by. There is no simulation with $L$ outcomes and $p_x$ treatments, correlated tests, a mix of null and non-null hypotheses, and e-BH applied to the ensemble.
    
    \reply The simulation study in our previous submission included $p_x=10$ covariates, with a mix of null and non-null hypotheses (corresponding to $\beta_i^*=0$ and $\beta_i^* = 0.15, 0.3, 0.5$). Since covariate values were generated from a correlated multivariate Gaussian, the corresponding point estimates $\hat{\beta}_i$ were also correlated, inducing a correlation between the tests being performed. This being said, we agree that it is interesting to consider a further simulation setup that mimics a real-life multiverse setting. To address your concern, we added a new simulation study inspired by the teenager well-being MCS dataset to which we later apply our multiverse framework.

The new simulation study (Section 4.2) considers 5 treatments and 14 control covariates.
Their values are generated by sampling with replacement from the MCS data,
so that the covariate correlation structure and the dependence between tests in our simulations match those of the MCS data.
We considered two sample sizes $n=1,000$ and $n=10,000$, and a moderately-sized multiverse with $L=5$ outcomes and a larger multiverse with $L=20$ outcomes, leading to a total of 4 simulation settings.
We then set generate values for each outcome from a linear regression model where some of the data-generating $\beta_i^*=0$ and some $\beta_i^* \neq 0$, so that there is a mix of truly active and truly inactive treatments.

We also applied the closed e-BH procedure (which has strictly higher power than e-BH) to the ensemble, as you requested.
Per your next comment, we also considered Benjamini-Yekutieli P-value adjustment as an alternative strategy to control the FDR.
Briefly, the power of the top two e-variable procedures was similar to that of BY adjustment, with slight advantages for the latter in the smaller multiverse and slight advantages for the former in the larger multiverse.
We observed similar findings in the teenager data, where BY rejected more hypotheses than closed e-BH.
This made us realize that the merits of e-variables for multiverse analysis lie not so much in FDR control, rather than on their flexibility in allowing to combine evidence across hypotheses and across independent datasets, and we updated the paper in this direction. 
In particular, the changed the title from ``evalues for multiplicity control in multiverse analysis'' to ``The role of e-values in multiverse analysis'', we updated the abstract, introduction and the rest of the paper accordingly, including an illustration of aggregating evidence with e-values in the teenager data in Section 5.2.
See also our next point below.



    \vspace{0.5cm}

    \item \textbf{Comment:} The obvious p-value-based competitor is missing. Benjamini-Yekutieli also controls FDR under arbitrary dependence and requires no nuisance parameter estimation. The paper compares e-value strategies only against each other. A comparison of e-BH against BY is needed.
    
    \reply Thank you for pointing this out, an assessment of the relative merits of BY vs. e-value strategies was indeed needed. Following your suggestion, we added BY to our simulations in Sections 4.2 and to the teenager data analysis in Section 5. We also added a detailed discussion to Section 3.2 offering some theoretical insights comparing BY to the e-BH procedure when the e-variables are based on p-to-e calibrators. 

Briefly, there are two main considerations. First, as pointed out by \cite{wang_ruodu:2022}, when the total number of tests $K$ being performed is large, e-BH avoids a $\ln(K)$ term in the denominator relative to BY. This implies that the power of e-BH scales slightly better as $K$ grows than that of BY.
Our new simulations in Section 4.2 reflect this. While the differences in power between the closed e-BH procedure (an improved version of e-BH) and BY are small, BY attains slightly higher power than closed e-BH in the smaller multiverse, and slightly lower power in the larger universe.

The second consideration is that, for any fixed $K$ the relative powers depend on the magnitude of the $k^{th}$ ordered reciprocal e-value $1/E_{[k]}$ versus that of the P-value $P_{[k]}$. For example, consider the mixture calibrator e-variable (which has a fairly competitive power in our experiments).
For small $P_{[k]}$, e-BH roughly seeks the largest $k$ such that
\begin{align}
 P_{[k]} (\ln P_{[k]})^2 \leq \frac{\alpha k}{K}
\nonumber
\end{align}
whereas BY approximately uses $P_{[k]} \leq (\alpha k)/[K (\ln(K) + 0.577)]$.
Therefore, if $(\ln P_{[k]})^2 > \ln(K) + 0.577$ then one expects BY to have higher power for rejecting these hypotheses, and otherwise e-BH has higher power. Because of the step-up nature of the e-BH and BY procedures, the hypotheses that are actually rejected are not driven by the most extreme P-values but rather by the smallest P-valuesatisfying the threshold.

As mentioned, both in our experiments and in the teenager data, BY-adjusted P-values has similar but overall slightly higher power than applying the closed e-BH procedure to calibrated e-variables.
Therefore, the main appeal of e-variables for multiverse analysis may lie in their flexibility to combine evidence across hypotheses and across datasets.
To illustrate this, we added a new Section 5.2 analyzing the teenager data. Therein, we obtain for each treatment an overall measure of evidence against the null hypothesis that the treatment has no effect on any outcome. We also illustrate how after having observed the e-values in the MCS data one can incorporate further evidence provided by an independent Monitoring the Future (MtF) study, while maintaining the statistical validity of each individual e-value. Such an exercise would not be possible with P-values because, after having looked at the MCS P-values, one has already spent the whole of the type I error.

\subsection*{Section 2}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]

\item \textbf{Commment:} Page 5. The subpopulation-encoding notation ($g_{ik}$) relies on a specific weighted sum-to-zero constraint. It would be useful for the authors to include a brief, intuitive explanation of how this constraint behaves in practice, as the current mathematical definition is quite dense.

\reply

We added an explanation how the encoding $g_{ik}$ guarantees that $\sum_{i=1}^n g_{ik}=0$, and an intuitive explanation that this condition simply ensures that the sub-population indicators are orthogonal to the intercept term. Equivalently, one could take the usual dummy indicators for sub-populations, do a least-squares regression of those indicators on the intercept term, and then our $g_{ik}$'s would be the residuals from that regression.
Because of this orthogonality, $\beta_j^{(l)}$ is an average population effect whereas $g_i^T \delta_j^{(l)}$ are deviations from that average, as shown two displays later in Section 2.

\vspace{0.5cm}


\item \textbf{Comment:} Page 5. Could you please clarify if the definition of treatment effect in terms of the derivative of $F\circ\mathbb{F}$ is mainstream or a specialized definition for your paper? Marginal effects are often defined in terms of the derivative of $\mathbb{E}$ (Ai and Norton 2003) instead, which accounts for the scale induced by the link function. Of course, this does not affect linear regression, but it induces a difference for other GLMs.

\reply We added this alternative definition of treatment effect alongside our original definition, and explained that it is particularly popular in the causal inference literature. Specifically, the treatment effect is $(F^{-1})' (x_i, z_i, g_i) [ \beta_j^{(l)} + g_i^T \delta_j^{(l)} ]$ and the ATE is $\beta_j^{(l)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (F^{-1})' (x_i, z_i, g_i)$, where $F^{-1}$ is the inverse link function.
We also explained that therefore testing for the existence of a treatment effect continues to correspond to testing $\beta_j^{(l)}=0$ as in our original defnision, and that testing for a treatment effect in a subpopulation continues to correspond to $\beta_j^{(l)} + g_i^T \delta_j^{(l)} =0$.
Since the rest of the paper focuses on hypothesis testing, which of the two definitions is used does not affect the rest of the paper much.


    \vspace{0.5cm}

    \item \textbf{Comment:} Page 6. The authors state control covariate effects ($\eta_z$) are always assumed non-zero, in contrast to SCA's subset approach. Could you please briefly justify why including all controls is universally preferable here, as over-controlling (e.g., inducing collider bias) can sometimes be a risk in observational multiverse data? Would a DAG or other tool help understand when this strategy is safe?

    \reply This was indeed not sufficiently clear. Our assumption is that the set of control covariates is a so-called valid adjustment set\citep{henckel:2022} that allows identifying the effect of interest. 
That is, one has already excluded any unwanted covariates that would alter the effect of interest, such as colliders or mediators, from the set of control covariates.
Under such assumption, the main issue is that some of the controls may not be needed because they are conditionally independent from the outcome, given the other covariates. Such controls are called instruments in the causal inference jargon.
Since including instruments in the outcome equation can lead to substantial variance inflation in the treatment effect estimates (for example, see \cite{papaspiliopoulos:2026}), a possible strategy is to attempt to identify and exclude them, and this was the strategy that we used in \cite{semken:2022}.
While this strategy leads to asymptotic power gains as $n \to \infty$ under mild assumptions, for finite $n$ one can make mistakes in removing covariates that are truly associated to the outcome (the so-called confounders), and hence one does not get an exact type I error guarantee. To avoid these issues, in this paper we assume that all controls are added, and hence that no confounders are omitted.

If one knows the DAG behind the causal mechanism the situation is much easier. Given such a DAG, \cite{henckel:2022} showed how to obtain the optimal set of adjustment covariates, which in particular excludes instruments and colliders. 
Briefly, one should only include as controls the parents of outcome and the parents of any mediators, excluding mediators and descendants of mediators.
By doing so, one avoids the mentioned variance inflation issues.

We added a discussion of these issues to Section 2. We also added a remark that, these considerations notwithstanding, we reiterate that our framework need not be framed within causal inference, and that it can be simply viewed as a strategy to study conditional associations (as we do in the teenager application).



\end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Comment:} The authors report different conclusions to the original studies. A slightly more extensive discussion on why that is the case would be welcomed.

\color{blue}
The main reason why our findings differ from the original study in \cite{orben:2019} is that the latter used Specification Curve Analysis to estimate a median treatment effect across all outcomes and treatments (as well as also across all possible control covariate configurations). As shown in Figure 5 there is significant heterogeneity of the effects across outcomes and treatments. For example, the point estimate for the association between high TV usage and most outcomes was negative (suggesting that TV usage might be associated with lower chances of poor mental well-being), whereas for social media and internet use the estimates were positive for self-assessed outcomes like depression and low self-steem and negative for parent-assessed outcomes like high peer problems and low pro-sociality. The median of these heterogeneous effects is close to 0, which led \cite{orben:2019} to conclude that the association between mental well-being and technology use was too small to warrant policy change. We argue that in the presence of strong treatment effect heterogeneity, summaries such as the median can mask the presence of important individual treatment effects.
We remark that there are observational studies \cite{valkenburg:2022}, randomized controlled trials \cite{allcott:2020,allcott:2022} and natural experiments \cite{braghieri:2022} indicating that there is a negative effect of social media on self-reported mental well-being, aligning with our findings.
